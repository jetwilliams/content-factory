# How tides work

> Example research notes shipped with content-factory for the dry-run demo.
> They are written to be accurate, but treat them like any research: check the sources yourself.

## Key points

- Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level, caused mainly by the Moon's gravity, with a smaller contribution from the Sun. [1][2]
- Gravity weakens with distance, so the Moon pulls the ocean on the side of Earth facing it a little more than it pulls Earth's centre, and Earth's centre a little more than the ocean on the far side. That difference stretches the ocean into two bulges: one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side. [1][2]
- As Earth rotates, a coastline passes through both bulges, so many places get two high tides and two low tides in about 24 hours and 50 minutes (a "lunar day"; the extra ~50 minutes is because the Moon moves along its orbit). [1]
- The Sun's tide-raising effect is a little under half the Moon's: the Sun is far more massive, but much farther away. [1]
- When the Sun, Moon and Earth line up (new and full moon), their effects add up and the tidal range is larger: spring tides. At first and last quarter they partly cancel: neap tides. "Spring" here has nothing to do with the season. [1][2]
- Coastline shape, ocean depth and basin size change tides a lot locally: some places get one high tide a day, some get mixed tides, and some bays have very large ranges. [1]

## Surprising details

- The "far side" bulge surprises most people: it exists because the far ocean is pulled least, not because something pushes it.
- Spring tides happen every month, about twice a month, not just in spring.

## Common misconceptions

- "The Moon lifts the ocean straight up." The vertical lift is tiny; the tide comes from the horizontal, tide-generating part of the force moving water around the globe.
